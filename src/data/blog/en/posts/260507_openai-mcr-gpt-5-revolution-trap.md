<div class="bluf"><strong>[BLUF]</strong><p> While OpenAI's MCR and GPT-5 offer unprecedented inference performance, they internally harbor significant risks, including strong dependency on specific clouds (Azure, OCI) and security vulnerabilities in Model Context Protocol (MCP) reference implementations. Infrastructure architects must prioritize evaluating 'MCP server vulnerabilities' and the transition costs associated with CSP lock-in over simple performance metrics.</p></div> <p> The field of Artificial Intelligence is currently heralding an era of unprecedented intelligence, driven by the wave of technological innovation led by OpenAI's MCR (Multipath Reliable Connection) and GPT-5. While brilliant performance metrics and future visions have heightened expectations across industries, behind these achievements lie technical debt and security gaps that CTOs and infrastructure architects—responsible for cloud cost optimization and data security—must carefully examine.</p> <p> Beyond simply praising performance, we aim to provide practical insights by highlighting the reality of specific cloud dependency forced by MCR and the security vulnerabilities of MCP reference implementations. Is OpenAI's innovation a path to a new horizon of intelligence, or a massive trap constraining corporate infrastructure? Let's take an in-depth look at what lies beneath the surface.</p><h2> The Reality of 'Efficient Dependency' Designed by MCR (Multipath Reliable Connection)</h2> <p> OpenAI's MCR is drawing attention as a next-generation network technology optimized for large-scale AI model training and inference environments. By utilizing 800Gb/s Ethernet interfaces and <a href="/en/glossary/what-is-srv6-segment-routing" class="glossary-tooltip" data-definition="A routing technology that specifies the data delivery path in the IPv6 packet header to increase network efficiency and flexibility, used in managing large-scale AI data center infrastructure.">SRv6 (IPv6 Segment Routing)</a>, it maximizes GPU efficiency and is known to reduce power consumption by connecting over 130,000 GPUs with just a 2-tier switch architecture.</p> <p> However, these technical achievements carry significant implications because they are optimized for specific supercomputer infrastructures, such as Microsoft's Fairwater and Oracle's Abilene. While MCR enhances GPU efficiency by extending RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE), it is technically deeply tied to the specific hardware stacks of Microsoft and Oracle. This can deepen 'Azure OpenAI dependency,' posing long-term infrastructure cost risks for companies maintaining a multi-cloud strategy.</p> <p> The key differences between MCR-based infrastructure and standard Ethernet-based infrastructure are as follows:</p> <blockquote> <p> MCR-based infrastructure utilizes a 2-tier (SRv6-based) network architecture, where cloud flexibility is tied to a specific CSP, intensifying vendor lock-in. Conversely, standard Ethernet-based infrastructure uses a 3 to 4-tier (traditional Leaf-Spine) architecture, enabling multi or hybrid cloud setups. Furthermore, due to the nature of SRv6, the sender holds the right to select packet paths in MCR, requiring a thorough review of potential bypasses of network security policies.</p> </blockquote> <h3> MS and Oracle Cloud Lock-in Hidden Behind 800G Networks and GPU Efficiency</h3> <p> An 800G network interface requires a sophisticated physical design that splits into eight 100G planes, increasing the likelihood of dependency on a specific hardware vendor's technology stack. For a company to fully enjoy the benefits of MCR, it may ultimately find itself deeply bound to Microsoft Azure or Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI) environments.</p> <p> This deepening cloud lock-in increases cloud transition costs in the long run, hinders flexible infrastructure strategies, and leaves the enterprise vulnerable to policy changes or price hikes by a specific CSP. When considering OpenAI MCR security, reliance on a specific cloud environment necessitates a cautious approach regarding business continuity and cost-effectiveness.</p> <h3> The Price of Layer Reduction: The Paradox of Complex Troubleshooting and Infrastructure Management Costs</h3> <p> MCR dramatically reduced latency by collapsing the switch layers to two levels. This is predicated on a 'packet spraying' method where network packets are distributed across hundreds of paths. While efficient in theory, it can cause unexpected complexity in actual production environments.</p> <p> In the event of a failure, it becomes extremely difficult to trace the exact point of the problem among the numerous paths where packets were distributed. This places an immense burden on infrastructure managers. Without specialized personnel, there is a high risk that operational expenditure (OPEX) within GPT-5 infrastructure costs will skyrocket. Infrastructure based on MCR, where troubleshooting difficulty is rated as 'High,' carries a much larger operational burden compared to traditional methods where standard RDMA monitoring is possible. </p>
